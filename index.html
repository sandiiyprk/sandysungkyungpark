01 — About

Building taught me what's around the software

"Rather than focusing solely on building software, I became interested in what happens around the software — how technology is integrated into real systems, organizations, and businesses to create meaningful outcomes."

I'm self-taught. Most of what I know about building technology came from independent software and web-development projects — some for school problems that genuinely needed solving, some just to see if an idea would work. Along the way I picked up AI APIs, databases, web applications, automation, and enough data analysis to be dangerous.

Building software taught me that creating useful technology involves much more than writing code. Once something is actually deployed — used by real students, in a real school, with real edge cases — the interesting questions stop being about syntax. They become: how was this technology selected? How is it implemented? How do users actually interact with it? How does an organization decide to adopt it, and how does data support that decision?

That shift — from building things to asking how technology is integrated into real systems — is what led me to Technological Systems Management at Stony Brook University | SUNY Korea. I didn't lose interest in coding. I got more interested in everything coding sits inside of.

AI-Powered Student Election Exit Poll

Data & Analytics Project Lead · Namyangju, Korea · Jul 2025

Problem
Traditional exit polling for a student election was slow and unsystematic, giving no reliable read on turnout or sentiment while voting was still happening.
Why it was initiated
As lead of the data and analytics team, I wanted to see whether a properly sampled, AI-assisted pipeline could produce a trustworthy real-time read — not just a faster version of an ad hoc poll.
Approach / Design
Designed a systematic interval sampling methodology (k = 10) against a population of 979 voters, then built a real-time web application to collect responses as they came in during voting.
Build
Built the collection app and integrated the Gemini API to run sentiment analysis on open-ended responses, feeding a predictive-modeling layer over the structured poll data.
Role
Data & Analytics Project Lead — owned the sampling design, the analysis pipeline, and coordination with the rest of the team on collection logistics.
Test / Challenge
Balancing statistical rigor (a consistent sampling interval) against the practical chaos of live voting-day collection, while keeping sampling bias low enough to trust the result.
Result
96 responses collected under the k = 10 protocol against a population of 979 — roughly 98% survey completion — feeding a live dashboard combining sentiment analysis with a predictive read on the likely result.
Evaluation
Compared model forecasts against the actual election result: a 13% prediction variance. Post-election analysis traced this back to specific sources of model error and demographic sampling bias.
Key finding
Cross-tabulation and driver analysis showed 44.8% of respondents prioritized policy platforms over candidate background or personal relationships.
Reflection
Good sampling design matters more than raw volume — and even a well-sampled model still needs honest post-hoc evaluation against what actually happened.
Links
Demo and repository available on request.
This was a student-built applied AI/data project inside a real school environment — not a professional election-forecasting system, and its results shouldn't be read as representative beyond the documented sampling protocol and collected sample.

Quantifying and Mitigating Prompt-Based Carbon Emissions in LLMs

Self-directed manuscript · under review
Research question
Does how a prompt is written change the computational — and estimated carbon — cost of an LLM's response, and can that cost be reduced without losing answer quality?
Methodology
Self-taught the Python needed to run the study, with no formal research mentor. Compared two LLMs across mathematics, coding, reasoning, and humanities tasks, varying correctness, hints given, step length, and repeated questioning, and estimated the computational impact of each response pattern.
Findings
Developed the concept of Green Prompting — using prompt design and model-path choices to reduce unnecessary computational cost without sacrificing answer quality. Under this study's experimental assumptions, response patterns were associated with an estimated cost on the order of 0.004 g CO₂ per token.
That figure is an estimate produced under this study's specific experimental assumptions — not a universal carbon footprint for any LLM token.
